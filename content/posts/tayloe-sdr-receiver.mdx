# Building a Tayloe SDR Receiver

I wanted a project that would force me to actually design analog rather than
read about it. Software-defined radio is a good forcing function: the front
end is unavoidably analog, the clocking is unavoidably digital, and if you get
either one wrong the failure shows up as something you can hear.

This is the first revision of a 2"x2" HF receiver board. Antenna in on SMA,
I/Q samples out over USB, all demodulation on the laptop. Below is how it
works and what I got wrong on the way to sending it out for fab.

![Block diagram of the receiver: SMA input through a 30 MHz low-pass filter into the CBTLV3253 Tayloe mixer, then a gain-31.6 preamp into a PCM1808 stereo ADC. A Si5351 25 MHz reference drives a 74LVC74 divide-by-four that clocks the mixer. The ADC feeds the RP2040 over I2S into PIO and DMA, and out to a laptop running GNU Radio.](/tayloe-block-diagram.png)

purple.

## The Tayloe detector

The core of the receiver is a Tayloe quadrature sampling detector. The idea is
elegant enough that it took me a while to believe it works.

You take the RF input and connect it, in rotation, to four capacitors — one at
a time, each for exactly a quarter of the LO period. Each capacitor integrates
the input over the window it's connected for. If the switching rate equals the
carrier frequency, capacitor 1 accumulates the signal at 0 degrees of phase,
capacitor 2 at 90, capacitor 3 at 180, capacitor 4 at 270. Differencing
0 against 180 gives you I; 90 against 270 gives you Q.

That's it. No diode ring, no transformer, no conversion loss in the usual
sense. It's a sampling mixer, and because the switch is on for a full quarter
period rather than an instant, it integrates rather than samples — which means
it also gives you gain instead of throwing away energy.

The switch is an `SN74CBTLV3253` — a dual 4:1 FET multiplexer. Nothing exotic,
just NMOS pass transistors with a decoder in front. Ron is roughly 7 ohms.

### Picking the sampling capacitor

The capacitor value sets the audio bandwidth directly. The switch presents the
source resistance to each cap for 1/4 of the time, so the effective RC uses
n = 4:

```
f_3dB = 1 / (2 * pi * n * R * C)
```

With a 50 ohm antenna and 100 nF caps:

```
f_3dB = 1 / (2 * pi * 4 * 50 * 100e-9)
      = 1 / (2 * pi * 2e-5)
      = 7958 Hz
```

About 8 kHz, which is right for AM and comfortable for SSB. I went with
X7R here. The usual objection is dielectric distortion, but these caps sit at a
fixed 1.65 V DC bias with only millivolts of signal on top, so they barely see
any voltage swing at all. Buying all four from the same reel matters more than
the tolerance spec, since it's the *matching* between the four that sets I/Q
balance, not their absolute value.

## Generating quadrature

The mixer needs four phases at the carrier frequency, which means the switch
control needs to run at 4x the LO. A `Si5351A` generates the master clock —
I2C-programmable, 25 MHz crystal reference, three outputs.

To turn that into two quadrature square waves I used a `74LVC74` dual D
flip-flop wired as a mod-4 Johnson counter. The Q output of the second stage
feeds back inverted to the D input of the first, giving the sequence:

```
(LSB, MSB): 00 -> 01 -> 11 -> 10 -> 00 ...
```

Two outputs, 90 degrees apart, both at exactly f_clk / 4. The divide-by-four
is the point: a Johnson counter's outputs are inherently in quadrature with a
phase relationship set by logic, not by matched delay lines. Phase accuracy
comes free.

Those two lines drive the mux select pins. Working through the truth table,
the sequence selects the four phase taps in the order 0, 90, 180, 270 — which
is the check I most wanted to get right, because getting it wrong doesn't
produce a broken board, it produces a board that receives on the image
frequency and looks fine until you measure it.

## Differencing and gain

Each I/Q pair goes into a difference amplifier. Gain is set by 1k input
resistors and 31.6k feedback:

```
Av = 31.6k / 1k = 31.6   (about 30 dB)
```

The closed-loop bandwidth requirement here is almost embarrassingly low. Noise
gain is 1 + 31.6 = 32.6, so with a 14 MHz GBW part:

```
BW = 14e6 / 32.6 = 429 kHz
```

against a signal band that stops around 3 kHz. Over 100x margin. Slew rate is
even less demanding — 3 kHz at 2 Vpp needs

```
SR = 2 * pi * 3000 * 1 = 0.019 V/us
```

which every op amp ever made clears by three orders of magnitude.

What actually matters is input voltage noise, referred through that noise gain
of 32.6, and the 1/f corner — because at a few hundred Hz you're often sitting
inside the flicker region where the datasheet's headline 10 kHz number is a
polite fiction. The resistors contribute too:

```
Rin thermal:  sqrt(4kTR) = 4.07 nV/rtHz, times 31.6  -> 129 nV/rtHz
Rf thermal:   4.07 * sqrt(31.6)                      ->  23 nV/rtHz
op amp:       en * 32.6
```

At 8.5 nV/rtHz the amplifier contributes 277 nV/rtHz and dominates both
resistor terms, which is the correct place to land — it means the resistor
values aren't the thing limiting you.

Everything is single-supply, so a 1.65 V mid-rail bias runs through the whole
analog chain. A separate op amp buffers a 10k/10k divider and feeds two
RC-filtered legs, one for the mixer common node and one for the amplifier
references.

## Digitizing

A `PCM1808` stereo audio ADC takes I and Q as its two channels — 24-bit, I2S
out. Using an audio codec as an SDR back end feels like a hack until you
realize the requirements are identical: two matched channels, DC to ~20 kHz,
90+ dB dynamic range.

I run it in **master mode**, with MD1 and MD0 both pulled high. The ADC
generates BCK and LRCK from its own system clock, which comes from a spare
Si5351 output. The RP2040 is therefore an I2S *slave* — its PIO block just
clocks data in on someone else's edges. This is worth writing down somewhere
your future self will find it, because bringing up PIO in master mode means two
chips driving the same two pins.

## Firmware, or the lack of it

The microcontroller does as little as possible. It is a dumb digitizer:

- PIO captures the I2S stream
- DMA writes it into a circular buffer
- A separate read pointer feeds the USB stream
- I2C tunes the Si5351

That's the whole firmware. No demodulation, no filtering, no AGC. All of that
runs on the laptop in GNU Radio, where I can change a filter by dragging a
block instead of reflashing.

This was a deliberate reversal. My first plan had AM and SSB demod running on
the MCU. Moving it to the host meant the firmware became something I could
finish in an evening and stop thinking about, and the interesting DSP became
something I could iterate on in seconds. On a first-revision board, the thing
you want most is for as much as possible to be changeable without a soldering
iron.

## What I got wrong

The design review process was more educational than the design.

**The package suffix is part of the part number.** I put `OPA2320` in the
schematic comment field with no suffix. JLCPCB's BOM matcher happily paired it
with `OPA2320AIDR` — the D suffix, SOIC-8, 1.27 mm pitch — against a footprint
drawn for DGK8, which is MSOP at 0.65 mm pitch. Nearly double the pitch. It
would have come back unbuildable, and the tool raised no warning, because from
its point of view it found an exact match for what I'd typed. Every line in a
BOM needs the full orderable part number, not the family name.

**Cheap parts exist and the expensive one is rarely necessary.** The OPA2320
pair was a third of my entire BOM cost. Working backward from what the circuit
actually needs — 2.5 V minimum supply, rail-to-rail I/O, under ~10 nV/rtHz,
and a bandwidth requirement I'd already shown was met with 100x margin — the
constraint that eliminated most candidates wasn't bandwidth or noise. It was
minimum supply voltage. A lot of attractive low-noise audio op amps stop at
4.5 V, which is useless on a 3.3 V rail. Filtering on supply range first and
taking whatever GBW fell out got the same performance for about a quarter of
the price.

**Assembly overhead can exceed the parts cost.** On a 5-board order my
components came to \$8.90/board while the assembly line item was \$24.79/board.
The gap is largely per-unique-part loading fees, and those are fixed costs —
they halve per board if you order ten. Some are avoidable for free by choosing
values that are stocked as basic parts; a resistor value that happens to be
E96 instead of E24 costs you a setup fee for nothing, since what matters in a
differential pair is matching, not the absolute value.

**Rotation conventions are a real failure mode.** I had a crystal placed at a
non-orthogonal angle — no functional reason, just a stray drag during
placement. Anything other than a clean multiple of 90 degrees compounds the
rotation-convention offset between CAD pick-and-place output and the assembly
machine. It costs nothing to straighten and removes a whole class of failure.

## Open questions for revision 2

Things I know are imperfect and shipped anyway, because a first spin is for
learning:

- The mixer moved from a 5 V part to a 3.3 V one, which halves the gate
  overdrive on the pass transistors. Ron roughly doubles and becomes more
  signal-dependent. I want to measure the conversion loss against the sim.
- The Si5351 shares a supply rail with the analog front end. A ferrite helps.
  It is not the same as a separate regulator.
- The LO routing crosses the board diagonally to reach the mixer, passing near
  the preamp summing nodes. A 3 ns edge has energy well past 100 MHz. Moving
  the divider next to the mixer is the obvious fix.
- The analog and digital grounds join through a single ferrite bead. Defensible,
  but the I/Q signals cross that boundary on their way to the ADC.

## Bring-up plan

When the boards arrive:

1. Power only. Check both rails and the bias voltage at the test points.
2. Scope the two quadrature clocks at the mixer. Confirm the divide ratio and
   the 90 degree relationship before trusting anything downstream.
3. Inject a known carrier and look for a tone at the difference frequency.
4. Check I/Q amplitude and phase balance, and compute image rejection from it.

Then the interesting part starts.
