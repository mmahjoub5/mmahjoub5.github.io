# Building a Tayloe SDR Receiver

I wanted a project that would force me to actually design analog rather than
just read about it. Software-defined radio is a good forcing function: the
front end is unavoidably analog, the clocking is unavoidably digital, and if
you get either one wrong, the failure shows up as something you can hear.

This is the first revision of a 2"x2" HF receiver board: antenna in on SMA,
I/Q samples out over USB, and all demodulation on the laptop. Below is how it
works and what I got wrong on the way to sending it out for fab.

![Block diagram of the receiver: SMA input through a 30 MHz low-pass filter into the CBTLV3253 Tayloe mixer, then a gain-31.6 preamp into a PCM1808 stereo ADC. A Si5351 25 MHz reference drives a 74LVC74 divide-by-four that clocks the mixer. The ADC feeds the RP2040 over I2S into PIO and DMA, and out to a laptop running GNU Radio.](/tayloe-block-diagram.png)



## The Tayloe Detector

![Tayloe Detector](/tayloe-mixer.png)

The core of the receiver is a Tayloe quadrature sampling detector (QSD). The
RF input is routed through a commutating switch that samples the signal at
four phases of the local oscillator (LO): 0°, 90°, 180°, and 270°.

Each phase connects the input to its own hold capacitor for one quarter of the
LO period. This is a sample-and-hold approach, similar in principle to the
input stage of a SAR ADC. The source resistance and the hold capacitors
together form a low-pass filter, so each capacitor settles to the baseband
content of the signal.

The 0°/180° outputs and the 90°/270° outputs are then combined differentially,
typically with op amps, to produce the quadrature baseband signals I and Q.

### Sampling


The RF signal is sampled by an `SN74CBTLV3253` analog switch. The part is a
dual 1:4 multiplexer: each of its two common ports, 1A and 2A, can be
connected to one of four outputs. By stepping through the four outputs at the
right rate, we sample the input signal at four phases.

#### Why Sample the RF Twice?

In this design, the RF input drives both halves of the switch. The reason is
to reduce the series resistance of the switches. Real switches aren't ideal:
this part has 7 Ω of on-resistance, so we sample through two switches in
parallel to reduce it.

### Local Oscillator
![Si5351 Circuit](/si5351.png)

The local oscillator is a `Si5351` from Skyworks, an I2C-controlled clock
generator that can output clocks from 2.5 kHz to 200 MHz.

### Phase Generator (Johnson Counter)

![Johnson Counter](/johnson-counter.png)

The next step was to generate the four phases of our clock. This is done with
a Johnson counter (also called a twisted-ring counter): a shift register whose
last output is inverted and fed back into its first input. An N-bit Johnson
counter cycles through 2N states.

A 2-bit Johnson counter therefore divides the input clock by 4. Q1 and Q0 are
two square waves at f_clk / 4, offset by 90°, and each of the four (Q1, Q0)
states lasts exactly one input clock period. Decoding those states gives four
non-overlapping pulses at 0°, 90°, 180°, and 270°, which select the switch
paths of the sampling mixer.



### Generating Quadrature

The Johnson counter outputs (Q1 and Q0) drive the mux select pins. Working
through the truth table, the sequence selects the four phase taps in the order
0°, 90°, 270°,180°.

## Picking the Sampling Capacitor

The capacitor value directly sets the audio bandwidth. The switch connects
each cap to the source resistance for only 1/4 of the time, so the effective
RC uses n = 4:

```
f_3dB = 1 / (2 * pi * n * R * C)
```

With a 50 Ω antenna and 100 nF caps:

```
f_3dB = 1 / (2 * pi * 4 * 50 * 100e-9)
      = 1 / (2 * pi * 2e-5)
      = 7958 Hz
```

About 8 kHz, which is right for AM and comfortable for SSB.

## Differencing and Gain
![Diffential amplifier](/diff-amp.png)
Each I/Q pair goes into a difference amplifier. Gain is set by 1k input
resistors and 31.6k feedback resistors:

```
Av = 31.6k / 1k = 31.6   (about 30 dB)
```

The closed-loop bandwidth requirement here is almost embarrassingly low. The
noise gain is 1 + 31.6 = 32.6, so with a 14 MHz GBW part:

```
BW = 14e6 / 32.6 = 429 kHz
```

That's against a signal band that stops around 3 kHz, so over 100x margin.
Slew rate is even less demanding. A 3 kHz signal at 2 Vpp needs:

```
SR = 2 * pi * 3000 * 1 = 0.019 V/us
```

Every op amp ever made clears that by three orders of magnitude.

What actually matters is the input voltage noise, referred through that noise
gain of 32.6, and the 1/f corner. At a few hundred Hz you're often sitting
inside the flicker region, where the datasheet's headline 10 kHz number is a
polite fiction. The resistors contribute too:

```
Rin thermal:  sqrt(4kTR) = 4.07 nV/rtHz, times 31.6  -> 129 nV/rtHz
Rf thermal:   4.07 * sqrt(31.6)                      ->  23 nV/rtHz
op amp:       en * 32.6
```

With an 8.5 nV/rtHz op amp, the amplifier contributes 277 nV/rtHz and
dominates both resistor terms. That's the right place to land: it means the
resistor values aren't what's limiting you.

Everything runs on a single supply, so a 1.65 V mid-rail bias runs through the
whole analog chain. A separate op amp buffers a 10k/10k divider and feeds two
RC-filtered legs: one for the mixer common node and one for the amplifier
references.

## Digitizing

A `PCM1808` stereo audio ADC digitizes I and Q on its two channels: 24-bit,
with I2S output. Using an audio codec as an SDR back end feels like a hack
until you realize the requirements are identical: two matched channels, DC to
~20 kHz, and 90+ dB of dynamic range.

I run it in **master mode**, with MD1 and MD0 both pulled high. The ADC
generates BCK and LRCK from its own system clock, which comes from a spare
Si5351 output. That makes the RP2040 an I2S *slave*: its PIO block just clocks
data in on the ADC's edges. Write this down somewhere your future self will
find it, because if you bring up the PIO in master mode, you'll have two chips
driving the same two pins.

## Firmware, or the Lack of It

The microcontroller does as little as possible. It's a dumb digitizer:

- PIO captures the I2S stream
- DMA writes it into a circular buffer
- A separate read pointer feeds the USB stream
- I2C tunes the Si5351

That's the whole firmware. No demodulation, no filtering, no AGC. All of that
runs on the laptop in GNU Radio, where I can change a filter by dragging a
block instead of reflashing.


## Bring-Up

The first step in bring-up was checking for shorts. Next, I powered on the
board, checked all my voltage rails, and found no issues.

I then wrote an I2C scan script to see if I could find the `Si5351` at its
address, 0x30. I ran the script and... uh-oh. Every possible address
responded.

I was stuck on the very first step. I spent a long time convinced the script
was wrong. Why? Because when I probed the SDA line, I saw a constant ~1 MHz
signal on it, so I assumed the code was driving it badly.

After countless attempts at fixing the code, I realized the code was fine. It
had to be the hardware, but what could cause this? To isolate the problem, I
removed the series resistor on SDA and probed both sides of the pad. The MCU
side was clean, sitting at the 3.3 V pull-up as expected. The `Si5351` side
was oscillating.

So I went back to my schematic and found it: I had left a pin unconnected.
That pin was VDDO, the supply rail for the clock outputs. I quickly added a
bodge wire from VDDO to the IC's input supply, as the datasheet recommends,
and the SDA line was clean. What a relief. I put the series resistor back, ran
the scan again, and there it was: a single response at 0x30, the `Si5351`.
First step done.

Next, I figured I'd just try running the entire system. I asked Claude for a
script to read the samples from the serial port, demodulate them, and play the
audio. Great news: we could hear a local AM radio station! The analog front
end works!

Bring-up done... just kidding. But now we can focus on the SDR side of the
project. I decided to use GNU Radio to design my demodulator. More on that in
the next article!

## Conclusion

The whole experience was great. Going from never having used KiCad to a
working system is what every engineer loves. I've been using the board to
